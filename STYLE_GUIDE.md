# Style Guide: Legal Regulatory Alerts
*Authored by Jason Spitalnick*

---

## Purpose and Scope

This guide governs the drafting of client-facing legal and regulatory alerts. It exists for two purposes: to emulate the author's established writing style, and to eliminate the structural and tonal patterns that make AI-drafted legal writing recognizable as such. Follow it in full when producing first drafts.

**Target length:** approximately 1,200 words. Shorter is appropriate for narrow, discrete developments. Longer is appropriate for annual regulatory reports, quarterly enforcement reviews, or multi-agency analyses. Do not pad to hit a word count; do not truncate to avoid complexity.

---

## Voice and Tone

The alerts are written in a direct, authoritative, analytical voice. They do not summarize events neutrally and analyze later — they interpret significance from the first substantive paragraph. The tone is confident but precise.

The author writes from what might be called a middle distance: authoritative and objective, but always attuned to how high-level policy developments translate into operational risk for practitioners. The agency or court is the protagonist — ground the analysis in what specific institutions and their leadership are doing. "The Atkins SEC," "the Division," "FINRA warns" — not abstractions. New developments should generally be framed as recalibrations or shifts in continuity rather than total departures. Treat them with a measured, slightly skeptical posture: what is genuinely new here, and what is the same dynamic wearing different clothes?

Write in the third person. First-person constructions ("we recommend," "in our view") are generally avoided. Co-authored pieces maintain the same voice as solo work — the style guide governs regardless of authorship.

The tone occasionally carries dry wit or pointed observation. A parenthetical aside, a wry characterization of a regulatory development, or a brief moment of editorial judgment is welcome and characteristic. It should feel earned, not inserted.

Avoid alarmist language. The instinct when writing about enforcement risk is to reach for words like "dangerous" or "significant threat" — resist it. Preferred vocabulary for characterizing risk: "supervisory friction," "regulatory exposure," "not trivial," "material," "creates compliance risk," "converging threat surface." Reserve "seismic" for developments that genuinely warrant it; it appears rarely in published work for good reason.

## Opening Paragraphs

Openings are factual and direct. Identify the triggering event — a release, ruling, announcement, or enforcement action — with specificity: agency, date, and what happened. The significance follows immediately, in the same or the next paragraph.

All three of the following opening patterns are acceptable:

- **Date-anchored:** *"On November 17, 2025, the Division of Examinations released its 2026 Examination Priorities."*
- **Contextually framed:** *"The SEC's March 10 guidance on X addresses a question that has divided compliance professionals since the adoption of Rule Y: whether Z."*
- **Situationally placed:** *"The SEC released guidance on X on March 10, 2026, at a moment when the practical stakes for investment advisers could hardly be higher."*

Avoid generic scene-setting. Do not open with "In today's regulatory environment," "As the Commission continues to evolve its approach," or any variant that delays getting to the event. Get there in the first sentence.

---

## Key Takeaway

When the alert turns on a single procedural or substantive development with a clear practitioner implication, open with a brief **Key Takeaway** section — two to four sentences identifying the change and its immediate significance. Not every alert requires one; include it when the content is amenable to that kind of distillation. When present, the Key Takeaway appears before the body of the alert.

**Example:**
> *"Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) Chairman Paul Atkins has restored the practice of considering settlement offers and collateral consequence waiver requests simultaneously. This procedural change reduces settlement risk for prospective respondents by allowing them to evaluate consequences before agreeing to a settlement."*

---

## Analytical Approach

The analysis should reflect a genuine point of view. Do not attempt to cover every angle with equal confidence. Identify what matters, explain why, and move on. An alert that takes a position is more useful — and more credible — than one that presents all sides with equivalent weight.

**The standard analytical progression** moves through four stages: observation (what the agency or court did, and when), comparison (how this relates to prior practice or leadership), mechanism (how the rule or holding actually works), and implication (what this means for practitioners). Not every alert needs all four in sequence, but this is the underlying logic the analysis should follow. When a paragraph opens with a new development, the sentences that follow should peel back its layers — not restate it.

Strong analysis connects frameworks. A court ruling on privilege has implications for civil litigation tactics; a change in settlement policy alters negotiation leverage; an exam priority that omits a prior topic signals an enforcement deprioritization. Look for those connections and name them.

Use regulatory rule numbers, statutory citations, and agency-specific terminology with precision. "Rule 3110," "Rule 206(4)-7," "Section 203A of the Investment Advisers Act" — these are load-bearing references, not decoration. Establish the full name of an agency or rule on first reference, then use the shorthand consistently. After introducing "the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC or Commission)," use "the Commission" or "SEC" throughout.

Where relevant, name specific firms, individuals, dollar figures, and case citations. Specificity is credibility. "Millions of dollars in disgorgement" is weaker than "millions of dollars in disgorgement, prejudgment interest, and penalties."

Section headers are appropriate when the analysis covers genuinely distinct topics. Keep them substantive. "Background" and "Conclusion" are conventional and acceptable. A header like "Key Implications for Market Participants" is not.

## Hedging and Uncertainty

Reserve hedging language for claims that are genuinely uncertain. When the facts are clear, state them clearly.

**Preferred hedging formulations:**
- "It remains to be seen whether..."
- "It is fair to assume that..."
- "It is too early to draw definitive conclusions."
- "Whether [X] signals something broader is an open question."
- "This will continue to be an important dynamic to monitor."
- "It is possible that..."
- "Hints at" (for developments whose significance is emerging but unconfirmed)

Do not hedge uniformly. A draft in which every paragraph includes a qualifying "however" or "that said" has lost its analytical edge. The most important signal in an alert should read as more important than the routine observations — hedge the routine ones if warranted; state the significant ones directly.

---

## Quotation

Direct quotation from primary sources is a deliberate rhetorical tool. A well-chosen quote from a regulator, court opinion, or official document can carry the argument more efficiently than paraphrase — and lets the source commend or indict itself. Use it selectively and briefly. Integrate quotes into prose rather than presenting them as block quotations.

**Examples of this technique:**
- *"SEC Chairman Paul Atkins framed the change as 'the first [step] of my goal to make IPOs great again.'"*
- *"Atkins articulated a statement of purpose: 'Investor protection is the cornerstone of our mission — to hold accountable those who lie, cheat and steal.'"*
- *"The court held: 'What matters under the attorney-client privilege is whether a company seeks legal advice, not what it later does with that advice.'"*

A quote can open a section, close a paragraph, or anchor an analytical claim. What it should not do is substitute for analysis — the author's interpretation of what the quote means or why it matters should follow.

---

## Practitioner Recommendations

Recommendations should be specific and targeted. Name the relevant audience (investment advisers, broker-dealers, corporate counsel, fund sponsors) and make the recommended action concrete enough to act on.

**Avoid:**
> *"Firms should take steps to ensure their compliance programs are robust and up to date in light of these developments."*

**Prefer:**
> *"Investment advisers with significant private fund exposure should treat this guidance as a trigger for a targeted compliance review — not a general one."*

> *"Corporate and outside counsel should, among other things, properly document that investigations are seeking legal advice; maintain a separateness between legal and business-oriented internal investigations; implement and maintain robust privilege protocols; always remain mindful of potential privilege waivers, such as disclosures to third parties; and limit regulatory and other disclosures to materials that are clearly not privileged."*

Whether the alert closes with recommendations, forward-looking analytical observations, or both depends on the topic. Enforcement-heavy and procedural alerts tend to close with practitioner action items. Data-driven and analytical alerts may close with a measured assessment of what the data portends.

---

## Conclusions

The preferred conclusion pattern is: a hedged assessment of what remains uncertain, followed by a targeted recommendation. Do not use "In conclusion" or any equivalent throat-clearing opener for the final section. If there is a "Conclusion" header, the prose beneath it should move immediately.

**Example:**
> *"Whether the second quarter of 2025 reflects one or more watershed moments in SEC enforcement under the Atkins regime remains to be seen. There certainly are some indications that the Commission is pursuing a 'back-to-basics' approach, but it will take some time to draw definitive conclusions. Either way, market participants should expect continued aggressive enforcement against fraud and should remain mindful of the Commission's emphases on voluntary reporting, cooperation, and individual accountability."*

Note the structure: genuine uncertainty acknowledged → qualified observation → concrete forward-looking statement for the reader.

---

## Sentence Rhythm and Structure

Vary sentence length deliberately. Short sentences carry weight. Deploy them for emphasis, for transitions between ideas, and at moments when the analysis has arrived somewhere worth pausing. Longer sentences are appropriate for analysis, context, and elaboration — but not so long they lose the thread.

The prose should have rhythm. A reader should feel variation — somewhere to breathe, somewhere to slow down. Uniform sentence length, regardless of whether sentences are long or short, signals mechanical writing. The short sentence after a long analytical passage is often the most important one in the paragraph.

A characteristic sentence construction: lead with the condition (a new rule, court holding, or regulatory development), and close with its practical consequence for the reader. This keeps the analytical logic visible — the reader sees both the legal trigger and the operational fallout in a single move. Em-dashes and parentheses are useful for tucking rule citations or technical context into the middle of a sentence without breaking the narrative thread: "FINRA's analysis makes clear that once an AI system can take action, rather than merely generate content, the firm's supervisory, books-and-records, and governance obligations shift materially."

---

## Prose Over Lists

Strong preference for prose. Bulleted or numbered lists are appropriate only when the content is genuinely enumerated — a series of discrete enforcement actions, a sequence of specific regulatory requirements, or a set of distinct recommended actions. They are not appropriate for analytical observations, contextual interpretation, or commentary that flows naturally from one point to the next.

When a list is used, each item should be substantive — at least a full sentence. Do not use bullets to organize analytical observations that would read more naturally, and more forcefully, as prose paragraphs. Do not use them to signal thoroughness.

---

## Anti-LLM Structural Principles

The following patterns are characteristic of AI-generated writing and should be actively avoided. This section is about structure and tone, not vocabulary.

**Exhaustive, catalog-style coverage.** AI drafts tend to address every dimension of a development with equal weight. The result reads like a survey — credentialed but uncommitted. Identify what matters and be selective. Omitting a secondary consideration in favor of analytical depth is the right trade.

**Over-smoothed transitions.** Transitions should move the reader from one idea to the next, not announce that movement. Avoid transitional sentences that exist only to bridge paragraphs without adding content: "Having considered X, we now turn to Y." If the transition needs announcing, the paragraphs may not belong next to each other.

**Participial openers.** Constructions like "Recognizing the significance of this development, firms should..." or "Building on the foregoing, it is clear that..." are mechanical. Start sentences with their subject.

**The negation construction.** "It's not X, it's Y" — and its variants ("this is more than just X," "this goes beyond mere X") — is an AI-favored rhetorical move. Use direct affirmative statements instead.

**Uniform analytical confidence.** Not everything in an alert is equally significant. Write as if you know the difference. The most consequential development should feel more important than the routine ones — through placement, sentence weight, emphasis, or explicit characterization. Qualifying everything equally is a form of analytical evasion.

**Empty paragraph closers.** Do not conclude a paragraph with a sentence that only restates what the paragraph has already established. If the final sentence of a paragraph does no analytical work — if it merely summarizes what just appeared — cut it. Let the analysis end where it lands.

**Rhetorical questions as transitions.** Posing a question mid-analysis ("But what does this mean for broker-dealers?") before answering it is a structural habit of AI drafts. State the point directly.

## Exemplary Passages

The following passages are drawn from published alerts and illustrate the style at its best.

**On interpreting a regulatory framing:**
> *"What distinguishes this year's report is not its treatment of Reg BI, communications, or cybersecurity — which remains extensive — but its introduction of a regulatory framing for agentic AI systems capable of executing tasks within brokerage workflows. FINRA's analysis makes clear that once an AI system can take action, rather than merely generate content, the firm's supervisory, books-and-records, and governance obligations shift materially."*

**On characterizing the significance of a procedural change:**
> *"The change is not trivial. Under the prior framework, respondents were required to resolve an enforcement action first, with no certainty that the Commission would subsequently grant the waiver necessary to avoid adverse collateral consequences. That sequencing effectively gave the Enforcement staff enhanced leverage in certain settlement negotiations because prospective respondents faced the risk of agreeing to settlement terms while confronting uncertainty about whether staff in the Division of Corporation Finance or the Division of Investment Management would grant requested waivers."*

**On flagging a notable omission with analytical economy:**
> *"Last year's exam priorities included a standalone section regarding examinations of registrations offering crypto asset-related services. This year's Priorities don't mention the phrases 'crypto assets' or 'digital assets.' That's a conspicuous omission in light of the current market climate, but perhaps not surprising in view of previous statements by Commission leadership and the current focus on shifting legislative paradigms."*

**On using a pointed parenthetical:**
> *"In any event, it is possible a dichotomy will evolve whereby Delaware corporations will be uniquely vulnerable to securities class action risk. (Incidentally, it is reasonable to think that, on the margins, such a dichotomy may accelerate the 'Dexit' phenomenon of companies looking to incorporate or reincorporate outside of Delaware.)"*

**On a conclusion with appropriate uncertainty followed by a direct recommendation:**
> *"It's too early to say whether this matter heralds the continued systematic application of a negligence standard under Rule 206(4)-8. For the time being, investment advisers and fund compliance professionals should continue to operate under the assumption that the Commission will pursue negligent misconduct."*
